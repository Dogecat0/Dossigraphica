## 1. Geographic Profile Summary

Advanced Micro Devices, Inc. (AMD) is a global fabless semiconductor company headquartered in **Santa Clara, California**, with a vast international footprint spanning subsidiaries in over **20 countries** across six continents. These include Armenia, Barbados, Belgium, Bermuda, Brazil, Canada, China, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Hong Kong, India, Ireland, Israel, Italy, Japan, Malaysia, the Netherlands, Poland, Romania, Serbia, Singapore, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom.

AMD generates **66–71% of its net revenue** from international markets. In FY2024, key revenue contributions by region were:
- **United States:** $8.7B
- **China/Hong Kong:** $6.2B
- **Singapore:** $3.6B
- **Taiwan:** $3.3B
- **Japan:** $1.8B
- **Europe:** $1.6B

The company employs approximately **31,000 people** globally and operates through four primary segments: **Data Center, Client, Gaming, and Embedded**. AMD serves a diverse customer base across **North America, Asia-Pacific, and Europe**, solidifying its position as a leading global semiconductor player.

### Geographic Diversification & Concentration Risk

International sales accounted for **66% of net revenue** in FY2024, underscoring AMD's significant geographic diversification but also its exposure to geopolitical and trade risks.

**China Exposure:** China (including Hong Kong) represents the largest international market at an estimated **24% of Q1 2026 revenue** (~$2.47B). This concentration carries notable risk given ongoing U.S. export controls on advanced semiconductors. In Q2 2025, AMD incurred approximately **$800M in inventory and related charges** after the U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) restricted exports of the AMD Instinct MI308 to China. Subsequently, BIS approved the MI308 for sale to China under a conditional framework where the U.S. government receives **15% of proceeds** from those sales. In Q1 2026, AMD recorded roughly **$100M in MI308 sales to China**, reflecting a partial recovery in that channel.

**Key Takeaway:** While AMD's revenue base is globally diversified, the heavy reliance on China (nearly a quarter of total revenue) creates a material geopolitical risk factor that investors should monitor closely, especially as export control regimes continue to evolve.

### 4.4 Geographic Concentration & Risk Assessment

- **Taiwan Dominance**: Three of five foundry partners (TSMC, UMC) and two of three ATMP partners (SPIL, KYEC) are based in Taiwan. TSMC is the sole supplier for AMD's most advanced nodes (7nm and below), representing a **critical single-point-of-failure** risk given geopolitical tensions in the Taiwan Strait.
- **China Dependency**: Tongfu Microelectronics (Nantong, China) handles the **majority** of AMD's ATMP needs, classified as critical. This introduces supply chain vulnerability amid US-China trade restrictions.
- **US & Korea Diversification**: GLOBALFOUNDRIES (New York) and Samsung (South Korea) provide alternative foundry capacity for mature nodes and memory, partially mitigating Taiwan concentration risk.
- **India Emerging Node**: HSMC in Hyderabad represents AMD's expansion into India for production, though currently rated standard criticality.
- **Sanmina (US)**: Provides domestic contract manufacturing for data center infrastructure, supporting AMD's post-ZT Systems acquisition strategy.

## 5. Customer Concentration

AMD's SEC filings explicitly disclose **Sony** as a major customer, representing **18% of revenue in fiscal 2024** (up from 16% in both 2023 and 2022). ## 6. Regulatory Risk

AMD operates in a highly regulated global semiconductor environment, facing a complex web of export controls, sanctions, trade restrictions, and compliance mandates. The regulatory landscape has become increasingly volatile, particularly with respect to U.S.-China technology competition, with direct and material financial consequences for AMD's AI chip business.

### 6.1 China Export Controls & Revenue Exposure

The most critical regulatory risk facing AMD is the escalating U.S. export control regime targeting advanced semiconductors destined for China. China accounted for **22% ($5,207M)** of AMD's net revenue in fiscal 2022, making it a strategically vital market.

| Risk | Region | Score | Impact | Key Detail |
|------|--------|-------|--------|------------|
| China Export Controls on MI308/MI325X | China | 5/5 | Critical | ~$800M inventory charges (Q2 2025); ~$440M net charges (FY2025); 15% revenue-sharing condition |
| THATIC Joint Venture Entity List Sanctions | China | 4/5 | High | AMD JV with Higon (Entity List); milestone payments & royalties at risk |
| U.S.-China Trade War & Retaliatory Tariffs | China | 3/5 | Moderate | Tariffs increase customer infrastructure costs, risking AI investment delays |

**China Export Controls (MI308/MI325X):** Since October 2023, BIS export controls have prevented AMD from shipping Instinct MI308 and Versal FPGAs to China without a license. The situation deteriorated significantly through 2025–2026:

- **April 2025:** New license requirements imposed for D5 countries.
- **Q2 2025:** AMD recorded approximately **$800M in inventory charges** on MI308.
- **Full Year 2025:** Net charges totaled approximately **$440M**.
- **August 2025:** AMD agreed to pay the U.S. government **15% of AI chip sale revenues from China** as a condition for the MI308 license — a provision raising constitutional concerns under Article I, Section 9, Clause 5.
- **January 2026:** BIS rule reviews MI325X on a case-by-case basis, maintaining uncertainty.

**THATIC Joint Venture:** AMD's equity interests in two joint ventures with Higon Information Technology (added to the Entity List in June 2019) remain at risk. AMD may not realize expected milestone payments and royalties. Additionally, China's SAMR imposed restrictive conditions on AMD's acquisition of Xilinx in 2022 as part of antitrust review approvals.

**Trade War Tariffs:** Worsening U.S.-China trade relations have led to retaliatory tariffs that increase data center hardware infrastructure costs for AMD's customers, potentially causing delays or halts in AI infrastructure investments.

### 6.2 Taiwan & Geopolitical Manufacturing Concentration

AMD's complete dependence on TSMC for all advanced-node wafer production (7nm or smaller) creates a single-point-of-failure risk of the highest order.

| Risk | Region | Score | Impact | Key Detail |
|------|--------|-------|--------|------------|
| TSMC Manufacturing Concentration & Taiwan Strait Risk | Taiwan | 5/5 | Critical | 100% dependency on TSMC for advanced nodes; earthquake risk (April 2024) |
| Entity List Additions in Taiwan | Taiwan | 3/5 | Moderate | 12 Taiwanese entities added to BIS Entity List (March 2025) |

**Taiwan Strait Risk:** Any disruption at TSMC due to geopolitical conflict between China and Taiwan could materially adversely affect AMD's business. The April 2024 earthquake in Taiwan demonstrated the physical vulnerability of AMD's third-party wafer foundries. AMD's Taiwan-based foundries, manufacturing facilities, and subcontractors are all exposed to geopolitical changes between China and Taiwan.

**Entity List Expansion:** On March 25, 2025, BIS added 80 entities to the Entity List, including 12 in Taiwan for developing advanced AI, supercomputers, and AI chips. This expands restrictions affecting AMD's supply chain and partner ecosystem in Taiwan.

### 6.3 U.S. Regulatory Volatility

The U.S. regulatory environment has been marked by significant policy reversals and persistent uncertainty.

| Risk | Region | Score | Impact | Key Detail |
|------|--------|-------|--------|------------|
| BIS Regulatory Uncertainty & Policy Flip-Flops | United States | 4/5 | High | AI Diffusion Rule (Jan 2025) → Rescinded (May 2025) → New Rule (Jan 2026) |
| Deemed Export & R&D Team Restrictions | United States | 3/5 | Moderate | Impacts non-U.S. personnel working on U.S. product roadmaps |

**BIS Policy Volatility:** The rapid sequence of regulatory changes creates persistent uncertainty for AMD's product roadmap and revenue planning:
- **January 2025:** BIS issued the AI Diffusion Rule.
- **May 13, 2025:** BIS rescinded the Biden AI Diffusion Rule and instructed enforcement officials not to enforce.
- **May 2025:** BIS announced intention to rescind and issue replacement rules.
- **August 29, 2025:** BIS closed the VEU program loophole.
- **September 2025:** BIS issued entity ownership rule (enforcement suspended until November 2026).
- **January 13, 2026:** BIS issued a new rule revising licensing policy for semiconductor exports to China.

**Deemed Export Restrictions:** These restrictions could affect AMD's ability to provide services or develop products in the U.S. with non-U.S. personnel. New export controls may adversely impact AMD's R&D teams located outside the U.S. from executing product roadmaps. BIS continues to expand licensing requirements worldwide for transactions involving entities and end uses associated with AI capabilities development and proliferation.

### 6.4 International Compliance Burdens

Beyond U.S.-China tensions, AMD faces a growing array of international regulatory obligations.

| Risk | Region | Score | Impact | Key Detail |
|------|--------|-------|--------|------------|
| EU AI Act & GDPR Compliance Burden | European Union | 3/5 | Moderate | Phased implementation; significant fines for non-compliance |
| Russia/Ukraine Sanctions & Shipment Suspension | Russia | 3/5 | Moderate | Shipments suspended March 2022; Xilinx had authorized resellers in Russia |
| Uyghur Forced Labor Act Compliance | Xinjiang, China | 2/5 | Low | Presumption of forced labor for goods from Xinjiang; increased compliance costs |
| Israel-Hamas Conflict Escalation Risk | Israel | 2/5 | Low | Broader regional instability could affect global supply chains |

**EU AI Act & GDPR:** The EU AI Act (adopted 2024, phased implementation) may impede AMD's ability to offer certain products and services in the EU. New and proposed AI legislation could impact AMD's ability to sell AI-related products and reduce its customer base. The EU GDPR imposes significant requirements on personal data collection and processing, with substantial fines for violations. Additionally, the EU has enacted restrictions on lead and other materials in electronic products affecting semiconductor devices and packaging.

**Russia/Ukraine Sanctions:** AMD (including Xilinx) suspended shipments to the Russian Federation during the fiscal quarter ended March 26, 2022. Xilinx had authorized third-party resellers in Russia filing notifications with the Russian FSB (a U.S.-designated blocked party under Executive Order 13382). Escalation of the Ukraine-Russia conflict could further negatively impact the global economy.

**Uyghur Forced Labor Act:** The U.S. Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act prohibits goods from the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region under a presumption of forced labor. Germany, the UK, Australia, and California have enacted laws requiring AMD to disclose forced labor and human trafficking elimination policies in its supply chain. These requirements increase AMD's compliance costs.

**Israel-Hamas Conflict:** While AMD does not have disclosed material direct operations in Israel, the broader regional instability could affect global semiconductor supply chains and financial conditions.

### Summary

The regulatory risk landscape for AMD is dominated by **U.S.-China technology decoupling**, which has already resulted in approximately **$1.24B in cumulative inventory and net charges** through 2025. The unprecedented condition requiring AMD to pay 15% of China AI chip revenues to the U.S. government sets a concerning precedent. Combined with complete manufacturing dependency on TSMC in a geopolitically volatile Taiwan, and persistent policy flip-flops from BIS, AMD faces a regulatory environment that is both financially punitive and structurally uncertain.
